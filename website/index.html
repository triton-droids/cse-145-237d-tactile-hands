01 — Overview

A robotic hand that knows when to stop squeezing.

Force-sensing resistors on every fingertip, calibrated to within 1 N, with a control loop that modulates grip in real time to grasp without crushing.

We built a low-cost robotic hand prototype with force-sensing resistors (FSRs) on the fingertips of all four fingers, capable of grasping and releasing simple objects while actively avoiding crushing them. The sensors were calibrated to within 1 N. Beyond the core demo, we explored vision-based finger pose estimation, VR teleoperation, and a sim-to-real pipeline. By the end of the quarter, the prototype performed basic grasping tasks on a small set of test objects, with the force-feedback loop actively modulating grip strength.

02 — Approach

Four phases. MVP delivered at week six.

We validated the sensor pipeline end-to-end before integration, then layered reach goals on top of a verified MVP.

Phase01

System design and component selection

Weeks 2–3 · complete

Finalized the hand platform (based on AmazingHand), selected the Pololu FSRs, chose the ESP32-S3 as the microcontroller, and defined the electronics architecture. Outputs: voltage-divider design, prototype board layout, calibration weight selection, and the initial simulation environment.

Phase02

Hardware build and sensor integration

Weeks 3–5 · complete

Assembled the mechanical hand, fabricated and populated the prototype sensor board, and attached FSRs to the fingertips. The ESP32 read force values over a voltage-divider network and streamed them over USB serial. We validated the pipeline end-to-end with synthetic values before connecting the real sensors. Calibration ran in parallel using 3D-printed weights, and the AmazingHand simulation came up alongside.

Phase03

Testing, calibration validation, and basic grasping

Weeks 5–6 · complete

Validated the calibration against known loads and demonstrated grasp-and-release cycles on simple test objects. The force-feedback loop confirmed that the system actively avoided crushing. This phase culminated in the MVP demo at week six.

Phase04

Reach goals and documentation

Weeks 7–10 · in progress

With the MVP delivered, we are now pursuing the stretch goals: VR teleoperation via Quest 2, vision-based finger pose estimation, sim-to-real object rotation, and mounting the hand on a robotic arm. The final report, video, and documentation will close out the quarter.

03 — MVP

Minimum viable product.

A robotic hand prototype with FSRs on each fingertip, calibrated to within 1 N, that demonstrates controlled finger motion and force-aware pick-up of at least one simple object.

  1. Operational hand prototype with tactile sensing Fingers open and close in a controlled way; FSRs are mounted on each fingertip and connected to the prototype board; the ESP32 reads them via voltage dividers and streams over USB; structure is mechanically stable.
  2. Calibrated force sensing to within 1 N 3D-printed weights map raw ADC to Newtons; non-linear curve fit with a polynomial; procedure is documented and repeatable across all fingers.
  3. Force-aware grasping The hand grasps at least one simple object while monitoring fingertip force in real time and adjusts grip strength to avoid crushing it.
  4. Repeatable test setup Force-aware behavior is shown multiple times; results are documented with video, force logs, and test notes.

05 — Specs

Build sheet.

Hardware
  • ESP32-S3 DevKit (WROOM-1) — 12-bit ADC, native USB, GPIO for four FSR channels and servo control.
  • Four Pololu FSR #2728 mounted to the AmazingHand fingertips.
  • AmazingHand mechanical platform with four Feetech SCS0009 micro servos.
  • Power — 2S LiPo (7.4V) stepped down to 5V via buck converter, Schottky diode to block USB backfeed.
  • Prototype board — solderable breadboard on the back of the hand, hosting the ESP32-S3, four 22kΩ voltage dividers, 100nF/10µF decoupling near the ADC, and 0.1" vertical headers for FSR Dupont connections.
  • Reference loads — 3D-printed PLA calibration weights at 0.5 N, 1 N, 2 N.
  • Reference objects — a plastic cup plus simple objects selected during testing.
Software
  • Firmware — Arduino core for ESP32, ADC sampling at 100 Hz, USB CDC at 115200 baud, CSV (timestamp + four forces in Newtons).
  • Simulation — MuJoCo or Isaac Sim.
  • Host — ROS 2 on Ubuntu; serial bridge node publishes /tactile/forces, subscribes to /servo/commands.
  • VR — Unity 2022.3 LTS, building the Quest 2 APK from leggedrobotics/unity_ros_teleoperation.
  • Vision — MediaPipe Hands (webcam fallback), ARKit HandPose (iPhone).
Tooling
  • UCSD Makerspace for fabrication and assembly.
  • Bambu Lab A1 Mini for 3D-printed parts and calibration weights.
  • Bench multimeter and oscilloscope.
06 — Testing

How we tested.

  • Calibration rig — a fixture held each sensor flat and level; weights (0.5 N, 1 N, 2 N, 4 N) were stacked sequentially with ADC logged over serial. Each sensor was cycled five times to characterize repeatability.
  • Reference test objects — a cup and additional simple objects, each with a known crush threshold defining grasp pass/fail.
  • Success criteria, met — error under 1 N across the force range; 5/5 consecutive grasp-release cycles per object without exceeding the crush threshold.
07 — Lessons

What we ran into.

  1. FSR accuracy was hard to hit within 1 N FSRs are nonlinear and drift with temperature and load cycles. We hit the target by calibrating against 3D-printed weights with a polynomial fit and characterizing repeatability across multiple load cycles.
  2. The PCB and wiring injected noise early on Analog FSR signals turned out to be sensitive to wiring length, grounding, and Vref stability. We fixed it by sizing the dividers properly, keeping analog traces short, and adding decoupling caps near the ADC.
  3. The hand had limited grip force Out of the box the AmazingHand servos struggled with anything bulky. We worked with light, simple objects and tuned the grasp geometry around what the platform could reliably hold.
  4. Reach goals spanned multiple subsystems We split ownership one-per-goal, developed them in parallel, and integrated only after the MVP had been validated — so a stalled reach goal never blocked the core demo.
  5. Time and hardware were tight We reused available parts wherever possible, kept the MVP as the gating priority, and refused non-essential dependencies until after week six.
