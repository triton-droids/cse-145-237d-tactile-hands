Force-aware grasping on the teleoperated arm — fingertip FSRs close the loop on grip strength, lifting the object without crushing it.

01 — Overview

A robotic hand that knows when to stop squeezing.

Robots without sensing are dangerous. Fingertip force-sensing gives a hand real-time grip awareness — yet no open-source hand under ~$200 has it. We built one.

We built a sub-$200 robotic hand prototype with force-sensing resistors (FSRs) on the fingertips of all four fingers, capable of grasping and releasing fragile objects while actively avoiding crushing them. The sensors were calibrated to within 1 N, and a real-time control loop modulates grip strength from the live fingertip readings. The hand grasps a paper cup and stops the instant measured force crosses a safe threshold.

On top of that core MVP, we layered vision-based finger-pose mirroring, VR teleoperation, and a MuJoCo simulation — then mounted the hand onto a teleoperated ARCTOS arm with YOLO-based object recognition. The sub-$200 figure covers the hand itself driven by a laptop camera; the Valve Index controllers and arm are stretch additions on top. The result is a reusable foundation for low-cost dexterous control.

02 — Approach

Four phases. MVP delivered at week six.

We validated the sensor pipeline end-to-end before integration, then layered reach goals on top of a verified MVP.

Phase01

System design and component selection

Weeks 2–3 · complete

Finalized the hand platform (based on AmazingHand), selected the Pololu FSRs, chose the ESP32-S3 as the microcontroller, and defined the electronics architecture. Outputs: voltage-divider design, prototype board layout, calibration weight selection, and the initial simulation environment.

Phase02

Hardware build and sensor integration

Weeks 3–5 · complete

Assembled the mechanical hand, fabricated and populated the prototype sensor board, and attached FSRs to the fingertips. The ESP32 read force values over a voltage-divider network and streamed them over USB serial. We validated the pipeline end-to-end with synthetic values before connecting the real sensors. Calibration ran in parallel using 3D-printed weights, and the AmazingHand simulation came up alongside.

Phase03

Testing, calibration validation, and basic grasping

Weeks 5–6 · complete

Validated the calibration against known loads and demonstrated grasp-and-release cycles on simple test objects. The force-feedback loop confirmed that the system actively avoided crushing. This phase culminated in the MVP demo at week six.

Phase04

Reach goals and documentation

Weeks 7–10 · complete

With the MVP delivered, we pursued the stretch goals: vision-based finger-pose mirroring (MediaPipe), VR teleoperation (after switching from the Quest 2 to Valve Index controllers), an in-hand cube-rotation policy trained in MuJoCo, and mounting the hand onto a teleoperated ARCTOS arm with YOLO-based object recognition. End-to-end teleop works. Sim-to-real transfer to hardware and finger adduction/abduction control remained out of scope.

03 — MVP

Minimum viable product.

A robotic hand prototype with FSRs on each fingertip, calibrated to within 1 N, that demonstrates controlled finger motion and force-aware pick-up of at least one simple object.

  1. Operational hand prototype with tactile sensing Fingers open and close in a controlled way; FSRs are mounted on each fingertip and connected to the prototype board; the ESP32 reads them via voltage dividers and streams over USB; structure is mechanically stable.
  2. Calibrated force sensing to within 1 N Stacked 3D-printed weights on a PLA plate map raw ADC to force; a power-law fit (F = 1.07e-9·r3.16) holds 17.1 g RMSE and 25.7 g max error over seven loading points — well inside the 1 N target. The procedure is documented and repeatable.
  3. Force-aware grasping The hand grasps at least one simple object while monitoring fingertip force in real time; when force crosses the safe threshold a red status LED lights and the grip stops closing, so the object is held without being crushed.
  4. Repeatable test setup Force-aware behavior is shown multiple times; results are documented with video, force logs, and test notes.

04 — Reach

Beyond the MVP.

Each reach goal was owned by one team member and developed in parallel, only integrating after the MVP was validated.

  1. Vision-based finger-pose mirroring · done A webcam MediaPipe Hands pipeline maps the user's per-finger curl to servo commands, and the robotic hand mirrors the gesture in real time.
  2. VR pose teleoperation · done We switched from the Quest 2 to Valve Index controllers — far easier to set up in Python — and stream controller position and orientation over ROS 2.
  3. Hand simulation in MuJoCo · done The AmazingHand was brought up in MuJoCo with verified finger kinematics and curl poses, and trained on an in-hand cube-rotation task.
  4. Teleoperated ARCTOS arm · done The hand was mounted onto an ARCTOS arm and driven end-to-end over VR teleop; we continue refining frame calibration, smoothing, and IK toward pick-and-place.
  5. Multi-object recognition · done A YOLO26 model fine-tuned on our custom-annotated dataset recognizes objects — the goal being to map each detected object to its own ideal force threshold.
  6. Sim-to-real & finger ab/adduction · not done Transferring the in-hand rotation policy to hardware and adding finger adduction/abduction control remained out of scope this quarter.

05 — Specs

Build sheet.

Hardware
  • ESP32-S3 DevKit (WROOM-1) — 12-bit ADC, native USB, GPIO for four FSR channels and servo control.
  • Four Pololu FSR #2728 mounted to the AmazingHand fingertips.
  • AmazingHand mechanical platform with Feetech SCS0009 micro servos — two per finger.
  • ARCTOS robotic arm — the hand mounts on the end-effector of the 3D-printed ARCTOS arm for teleoperated pick-and-place.
  • Power — 2S LiPo (7.4V) stepped down to 5V via buck converter, Schottky diode to block USB backfeed.
  • Prototype board — solderable breadboard hosting the ESP32-S3, four 22kΩ voltage dividers, 100nF/10µF decoupling near the ADC, and 0.1" vertical headers for FSR Dupont leads, housed in a 3D-printed enclosure with status LEDs on the back of the hand.
  • Calibration weights — stacked 3D-printed PLA weights held on a printed plate for seven-point loading.
  • Reference object — a paper cup, plus additional simple objects selected during testing.
Software
  • Firmware — Arduino core for ESP32, ADC sampling at 100 Hz, USB CDC at 115200 baud, timestamped force streaming.
  • Simulation — MuJoCo; AmazingHand model with verified finger kinematics, trained on an in-hand cube-rotation task.
  • Host — ROS 2 on Ubuntu; serial bridge node streams fingertip forces and relays servo commands.
  • VR — Valve Index controllers via OpenVR / pyopenvr, streaming controller pose over ROS 2 (chosen over the Quest 2 APK for easier Python integration and native per-finger tracking).
  • Vision — MediaPipe Hands + OpenCV (webcam) for finger-pose mirroring; YOLO26 fine-tuned on a custom-annotated dataset for multi-object recognition, with the aim of mapping each detected object to its own force threshold.
Tooling
  • UCSD Makerspace for fabrication and assembly.
  • Bambu Lab A1 Mini for 3D-printed parts and calibration weights.
  • Bench multimeter and oscilloscope.
06 — Testing

How we tested.

  • Calibration rig — a 3D-printed plate held stacked PLA weights over the sensor while the ESP32 logged ADC over serial. Seven loading points were fit with a power law (F = 1.07e-9·r3.16), giving 17.1 g RMSE and 25.7 g max error.
  • Reference test objects — a paper cup and additional simple objects, each with a crush threshold defining grasp pass/fail.
  • Success criteria, met — error under 1 N across the force range; the hand repeatedly grasped and released the cup, stopping the moment fingertip force crossed the safe threshold.
07 — Lessons

What we ran into.

  1. FSR accuracy was hard to hit within 1 N FSRs are nonlinear and drift with temperature and load cycles. We hit the target by calibrating against stacked 3D-printed weights with a power-law fit and characterizing repeatability across multiple load cycles.
  2. The board and wiring injected noise early on Analog FSR signals turned out to be sensitive to wiring length, grounding, and Vref stability. We fixed it by sizing the dividers properly, keeping analog traces short, and adding decoupling caps near the ADC.
  3. Force sensing had latency The grasp loop reacts to measured force, not anticipated force, so a fast close can overshoot the safe threshold. We mitigated it by moving slowly during the closing phase.
  4. The hand had low reachability The AmazingHand's reach and grip envelope are limited, so it struggled with smaller objects. We tuned the demos around larger objects the platform could reliably wrap around, like a paper cup, which were much easier to grasp.
  5. The Quest 2 was hard to integrate The Quest 2 APK pipeline was awkward to drive from Python. We switched to Valve Index controllers, which set up far more easily via OpenVR and expose native per-finger tracking.
  6. Reach goals spanned multiple subsystems We split ownership one-per-goal, developed them in parallel, and integrated only after the MVP had been validated — so a stalled reach goal never blocked the core demo.
  7. Time and hardware were tight We reused available parts wherever possible, kept the MVP as the gating priority, and refused non-essential dependencies until after week six.
